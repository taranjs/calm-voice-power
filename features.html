In brief

Four things worth knowing before you tap anything.

🗣️

It listens to the voice, not the words

No speech recognition anywhere. A repetition, a prolongation or a block costs the child nothing, because the app has no concept of a wrong word.

🎤

Nothing can be earned in silence

Every scoring activity is gated on the microphone actually hearing the child. A number that went up means they spoke.

🚫

Fluency is never scored

Not once, anywhere. The targets are things a child can consciously change — and two whole features are scored not at all, on purpose.

🔒

Recordings stay on the device

No account, no server, no analytics. A child's voice is about as sensitive as data gets.

And what it is not

It is not an assessment, it produces no severity score, and it is not a fluency-shaping programme. It cannot tell you whether a child is stuttering more or less this month.

It is the bit between sessions — the daily few minutes a clinician prescribes and a family then has to somehow make happen, sixty times before the next appointment.

The tour 🗺️

Twenty features in eight groups. Tap any screen to read why it exists without losing your place — then use the arrows to walk through the rest.

🎤 First, it learns their voice

1

A fixed loudness threshold is the quiet failure that kills voice-driven apps. Set it too high and a softly spoken child is ignored no matter how hard they try; set it too low and room noise earns their rewards for them. Neither failure is visible to the child — they simply decide the app is broken.

🎤 Teach Me Your Voice

Why it is hereThe app needs to know what this child sounds like, and how noisy their room is, before it can judge anything at all. Asking a parent to nudge a sensitivity slider was never going to happen.
What it isFour short steps played as a game rather than presented as settings: a mouse voice, a normal voice, a lion roar, and one gently started word. It measures the room’s noise floor and the child’s own quiet and loud levels, then places its detection threshold between the room and their softest voice.
What it givesDetection that works for a quiet child. In testing this moved one child from a marginal margin to a comfortable one — the difference between an app that responds and an app that appears broken. Re-runnable any time from the parent dashboard, which matters as a child grows or moves rooms.

🎮 Four games, four different targets

4

None of them scores fluency. Each gives immediate, visible feedback on a physical behaviour a child can consciously change — which is the only kind of target worth setting for a six-year-old.

🌱 Gentle Start

Why it is hereEasy vocal onset — beginning a word softly instead of forcing it out from a closed larynx — is one of the very few techniques a young child can genuinely feel themselves doing.
What it isA word appears and the child says it. The app measures how gradually their voice arrived and shows it back to them. Because no synthesised voice can demonstrate a soft onset, the app plays a purpose-built tone that swells gently, so there is something to imitate.
What it givesThe one number in the app that tracks a real speech target. The goal ratchets upward from the child’s own recent measurements rather than sitting at a fixed ideal, so it is always just above where they actually are.

🌈 Stretchy Speech

Why it is hereProlonged, continuous voicing is incompatible with a block. Practising the sensation of a sound that keeps going builds the alternative to pushing.
What it isThey hold a sound; the app tracks how long the voice stays continuously on, tolerating brief natural gaps rather than snapping off at the first flicker.
What it givesA personal best they can beat, measured in tenths of a second and entirely their own. Nobody else’s score is on the screen.

⏸️ Pause Power

Why it is hereTime pressure is the enemy. A child who believes a gap in their speech is a failure will rush, and rushing makes stammering worse. So the app makes deliberate silence the thing that wins.
What it isThey speak, then stop. The app times the real silence between two real stretches of speech and rewards the length of the pause.
What it givesReframing. A pause stops being something to be ashamed of and becomes a superpower with a high score attached to it.

🦕 My Buddy

Why it is hereEverything else here is about speaking more easily. None of it addresses how a child feels about getting stuck — and avoidance and shame do more long-term damage than the disfluency itself. This is the acceptance and desensitisation side, which almost no children’s app attempts.
What it isA named creature who stammers, is entirely fine about it, and needs the child’s help. It blocks on a word and they choose how to help. Every option is valid — the screen says so — and one of them is deliberate, playful bouncing on a sound, which is voluntary stuttering introduced as a game.
What it givesThe buddy’s lines are the actual payload: “I got a bit stuck… and I said it anyway!” and “Thanks for waiting for me — people who wait are the best.” The second quietly teaches a child what they are allowed to ask of a listener. Being the helper rather than the helped is the point: it rehearses a stance towards their own speech.

🧰 Tools for the moment itself

3

Practice is not much use if none of it is reachable when a word is actually stuck.

✨ Word Stretch

Why it is hereA direct rehearsal of what to do with a word that will not come: breathe, slow down, stretch it out.
What it isThe letters of the word physically pull apart as the child sustains the sound. Their voice is doing the animating — this was once a button that animated itself and paid out coins for being watched, which is exactly the bug the whole app was rebuilt to remove.
What it givesCause and effect they can see. Longer voicing, wider letters, no interpretation needed.

🌬️ Calm Breath

Why it is hereTension is upstream of everything else. Three guided minutes before practice changes the body a child is about to speak with, and gives them something to do when they feel the tightening start.
What it isAn animated breathing guide, roughly three minutes. No microphone needed.
What it givesA portable skill, and the one activity that still works when a child is too upset to want to talk at all.

🎵 Pacing Dots

Why it is hereSyllable-timed and rhythmic speech is long-established practice material. The catch is that it is trivially fakeable — a child can tap along in complete silence.
What it isA row of dots lights in rhythm, and a beat only counts if they actually voiced it.
What it givesRhythm and a slowed rate, with no way to earn it by tapping.

✍️ Their own words, in their own voice

2

Autonomy is one of the three things that make any activity intrinsically motivating — the others being competence and connection. An app that only ever hands a child someone else’s word list is leaving the cheapest motivation there is on the table.

✍️ My Words

Why it is hereThe words that matter are the ones that trip a child up: their own name, their teacher’s name, the thing they have to say at the school gate. A generic list cannot know them and a clinician’s list is only updated fortnightly.
What it isThe child adds their own difficult words and records themselves saying each one well. Those words then flow into the practice games, and their own recording becomes the model the app plays back — falling back to a synthesised voice only where they have not recorded one.
What it givesTwo things at once: practice on material that actually matters, and the experience of hearing their own voice held up as the good example. They are not copying an adult; they are copying themselves on a good day.

🎲 Content that doesn’t run out

Why it is hereThe original app had a fixed set of about sixty-five items, exhausted in weeks. Nothing erodes a daily habit faster than a child recognising every screen.
What it isWords and prompts are generated from word banks, seeded by the date: the same material all day so they can come back to a word they want another go at, different material tomorrow.
What it givesAn app that stays new without an adult loading content into it. The clinical constraints live in the banks rather than being left to chance — the gentle-onset words all begin with vowels, and the stretching words are built on sounds that can physically be prolonged.

💛 Other people — where transfer happens

2

Fluency in a game and fluency in a conversation are different skills. There is normally a chasm between practising words alone and talking to a real human, and the app used to have nothing in it at all.

💛 Talk Together

Why it is hereConversation is where communicative pressure does the most damage, and it is also the only place practice becomes useful. It needs a rung on the ladder of its own.
What it isStructured turn-taking with a real person in the room — a parent, sibling, grandparent or clinician. The app supplies the prompts and keeps the turns moving.
What it givesA scaffolded conversation. It counts turns taken and nothing else, deliberately: the aim is that speaking with someone stays entirely consequence-free. It also works completely with the microphone denied, because the microphone is not the point here.

🌍 Real Challenges

Why it is hereAvoidance is the habit that hardens over a lifetime, and small, chosen, real acts of speaking are how it is loosened. Those belong outside the app.
What it isDaily suggestions of brave everyday tries — ordering their own drink, answering the phone, asking a shopkeeper a question — which the child marks off themselves.
What it givesPractice that leaves the screen. Credit is for the attempt, never for how it sounded.

📈 Making progress visible to them

3

A child can improve for weeks and have no idea. One who cannot perceive their own progress has no evidence any of this is worth doing — and coins are not evidence, because they know coins are made up.

🎧 Voice Journal

Why it is hereThe most convincing proof available is the sound of their own voice a month ago.
What it isRecordings are kept and grouped by prompt, so the same sentence from the earliest attempt and the latest sit side by side to be played back to back.
What it givesAudible progress — for the child, and for the adult who needs to know whether any of this is working. This is also the part families most want to keep, which is why the app takes storage seriously.

⚡ My Voice Powers

Why it is hereRaw milliseconds mean nothing to a child. Levelling up a named power means a great deal.
What it isFour powers — Gentle Start, Long Sound, Pause Power and Steady Beat — each levelling up from the child’s own real measurements, described in words they can act on rather than numbers.
What it givesLegible competence. What grows here is genuinely their own performance, not a participation trophy, and that distinction is what makes it feel worth something.

🎙️ Record Me!

Why it is hereMany children who stammer have never simply listened to themselves somewhere nothing is at stake.
What it isThey record a prompt, play it back and keep it. No analysis and no score.
What it givesSelf-monitoring without judgement, and the raw material for Then vs Now.

🔁 Coming back tomorrow

3

The hardest problem in the whole app, and where the first version failed worst.

🛣️ Confidence Road

Why it is hereThe original streak punished a missed day by destroying the record, which made the streak frightening. Illness, holidays and bad days are not lapses in commitment; a design that treats them as failures teaches a child that consistency is impossible for them.
What it isA cumulative road of every day ever practised — it only ever grows. Alongside it, a current streak protected by rest credits: practise consistently and the app banks days off, so a missed day spends a credit before it breaks anything.
What it givesA record that is safe to care about. A child can come back after a fortnight away and find their history intact — which is exactly the moment most apps lose someone for good.

😄 Emotion check-in

Why it is hereHow a child feels about talking is the outcome that matters most and is the least measurable. Asking either side of practice makes it visible — and asking at all tells them their feelings are part of this.
What it isA five-face scale before practice, and the same scale again afterwards.
What it givesA trend line the adults can see, and a moment of being asked. In testing, practice reliably ended on a better face than it started — a pattern worth showing a parent who is wondering whether the effort is paying off.

🪙 Coins, Shop and Avatar

Why it is hereExtrinsic reward gets a child through the door in week one, and it is the weakest motivator here — offered too freely it actively erodes the interest it was meant to create. So it is present, capped, and never the main event.
What it isCoins for voiced practice, spent on unlockable avatars and customisation. Earnings taper per activity per day, so grinding one favourite game stops paying and breadth is rewarded. A first-practice-of-the-day bonus does most of the remaining work.
What it givesA gentle pull towards turning up, without teaching a child that speaking is something one does for payment. Several of the newest features pay nothing at all, on purpose.

👨‍👩‍👧 For the adult in the room

2

A parent or clinician needs three things: is the child actually using it, is anything moving, and what should I do differently.

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent Dashboard

Why it is hereThe original dashboard was fed by data that was never recorded, so it showed a confident and completely empty picture. A dashboard that cannot be trusted is worse than no dashboard.
What it isActivities per day for the past week, the before-and-after emotion trend, a gentle-starts trend, recent sessions, a rotating coaching tip, and a plain statement of where the child’s data is stored and whether it is protected from being cleared.
What it givesAn honest picture, including when it is flat. It also holds the coaching tips, because most of the variance in a child’s day sits with how the adults around them respond — and much of that advice runs against a parent’s instincts.

📈 The Gentle Starts panel

Why it is hereEverything else on the dashboard measures engagement. This is the only panel that tracks the actual speech target, so it is the one a therapist will want to look at.
What it isMedian onset rise time in milliseconds — how long the voice takes to climb from 10% to 90% of its level at the start of a word — shown as when the child began versus recently, against their current goal.
What it givesHonesty about a slow measure. When nothing has moved it says “holding steady for now — this one moves slowly, and that is normal” rather than inventing progress. A panel that flattered this number would be useless to a clinician.

Why it was rebuilt 📖

The design of this app is not a feature list someone brainstormed. It is a list of repairs — which is why the choices above are so specific.

It was built by a parent for their own six-year-old son, who stammers. The first version had all the obvious ingredients: breathing exercises, a coin economy, an avatar to unlock, a streak counter, a set of practice games. He loved it for about a fortnight.

Then he stopped opening it. He never said why — children rarely do. He just drifted off, and the app sat on the home screen going stale.

Rather than adding more features, the next step was working out precisely what had gone wrong. Five things had, and each is a trap any practice app can fall into.

The five things that were wrong
  • The rewards had nothing to do with speaking. He worked out he could earn the entire shop by tapping buttons in silence. The app was paying him for compliance, not for using his voice.
  • The streak was punitive. One missed day wiped out weeks of work. For a child that converts a source of pride into a source of dread — and the safest way to stop losing a streak is to stop caring about it.
  • Nothing came back from the adults. He practised into a void. The parent dashboard existed but was fed by data that was never actually recorded.
  • The content ran out. Around sixty-five fixed items. He had seen all of them.
  • Progress was invisible. He had genuinely improved and there was no way for him to hear or see it. The only visible number was coins, which he already knew were meaningless.

That diagnosis became the specification. Every feature in the tour traces back to one of those five failures, or to a clinical target worth aiming at once a child is actually turning up.

The question every feature is judged against: does this give the child a reason to open the app tomorrow, while practising something that helps? A feature that only satisfies the second half is a feature nobody uses.

Two big decisions 🔑

These explain most of what looks unusual about the app, and neither should be quietly reversed by anyone extending it.

1. Amplitude, not speech recognition

The obvious way to build a speech app is speech recognition. This app deliberately does not use it.

Speech recognition scores word accuracy. It treats repetitions, prolongations and blocks as garbage input. Pointing that at a child who stammers builds exactly the wrong feedback loop — the app would systematically tell a child they were failing at the precise moments they most needed encouragement.
What it measures instead

The app analyses the shape of the sound using the browser's audio analyser. Three things, none of which care what word was said or whether it came out cleanly:

  • Is the voice on? Loudness against a threshold calibrated to this child in this room.
  • How long is it sustained? Continuous voicing, for the breath and stretching work.
  • How gently does a word begin? The time the sound takes to rise from 10% to 90% of its level — a proxy for hard versus easy vocal onset. A hard glottal attack rises in roughly 15 ms; an easy onset takes 110 ms or more. Crucially it is independent of loudness, so a shy child and a loud child are judged on the same thing.

The microphone is also acquired with the browser's noise suppression and automatic gain control switched off, because both actively fight the thing being measured.

2. Nothing can be earned in silence

Every scoring activity is gated on the microphone actually hearing the child. Coins arrive because a sound was made and held, not because a button was pressed. The animation of a word stretching apart is driven by their own sustained voicing — it will not move if they watch in silence.

This is the single most important repair, and it also protects the parent dashboard: a number that went up means the child spoke.

The corollary — the microphone is never a wall. If mic access is refused or unavailable, the app still opens and everything that does not need a mic still works fully. A child should never be locked out of their own practice app by a permission dialog.

Never measured 🚫

Some of the omissions are the design.

  • Word accuracy or intelligibility. No speech recognition anywhere.
  • Fluency, in any form. Nothing counts disfluencies and nothing scores how smoothly a word came out.
  • Conversation quality. Talk Together counts turns and stops there.
  • Anything at all in My Buddy. Attaching a reward to the acceptance work would turn a stance into a performance.
  • Comparison with other children. No leaderboard, no age norm, no percentile. Every measure is against the child’s own past.

The tone rules follow from this. No red, no buzzers, no failure sounds anywhere in the app. Getting stuck is drawn as ordinary and survivable rather than as an error state. Praise is for effort and for turning up — "I noticed you kept going" — and never for fluency.

Your data 🔒

Short version: on the device, and nowhere else.

  • No account, no sign-up, no server. There is nothing to log in to.
  • Recordings never leave the device. They are stored in the browser's own database on that one device and are never uploaded, transmitted or analysed elsewhere.
  • No analytics, no tracking, no third-party scripts.
  • Works fully offline once loaded, which is also why none of this needs a connection.

The honest caveat. Because everything is local, everything is also only on that one device. Browsers can clear this kind of storage after a stretch without opening the app — taking the streak and the recordings with it. The app asks the browser for permanent storage at startup, and adding it to the home screen makes eviction far less likely; the parent dashboard reports which state you are actually in. But there is currently no sync and no cloud backup, so a lost or wiped device means lost recordings. Worth knowing before a family invests months of Then-vs-Now in it.

For speech and language therapists 🩺

If you are considering pointing a family at this, here is what to look at and what to be wary of.

A ten-minute tour of the real thing

  1. Run Teach Me Your Voice first. Nothing else behaves properly until it has calibrated, and it is the piece most likely to need re-running in a noisy clinic room.
  2. Play Gentle Start a few times — once with a hard attack, once with a deliberately eased onset — and watch what it reports. This is the app's only real speech measure and you should satisfy yourself it responds sensibly.
  3. Open My Buddy. It will tell you fastest whether the app's attitude to stammering matches yours.
  4. Take a couple of turns in Talk Together. This is the part designed to be used with someone, and the part most likely to be skipped if nobody points it out.
  5. Finish in the Parent Dashboard so you know what the family will see, and can tell them which parts to ignore.

How to read the one clinical number

The Gentle Starts panel reports median onset rise time in milliseconds — how long the voice takes to climb from 10% to 90% of its level at the start of a word.

What the goal tracks

It is computed from a rolling window of the child's own samples, and the goal sits just above their recent median rather than at a fixed ideal. Treat it as a practice-behaviour measure, not an outcome measure: it tells you whether they are rehearsing easy onset, not whether their stammering has changed.

Known limits, stated plainly
  • A single amplitude threshold cannot separate a child's voice from a sibling shouting nearby. In a busy room, credit can be earned by the room.
  • Onset rise time is a proxy. It is a good one, it is loudness-invariant, and it is not a laryngoscope.
  • The content banks are English and the child-facing copy assumes a British-English register.
  • The reading load is pitched at roughly ages 5–10 and the visual register is young; an older child may find it babyish.
  • No sync, no multi-device, no clinician view, no data export yet. What is on the child's device is all there is.
  • It has been shaped around one child in depth rather than trialled across a caseload. That is a real limitation, and the main reason clinician feedback is worth more than another feature.

What would help most. Whether the targets are the right ones, whether anything here would work against what you do in session, and whether the buddy's framing of getting stuck sits right with you. Those are the judgements this app cannot make for itself.
