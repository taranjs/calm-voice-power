Calm Voice Power

Why each feature exists

A daily speech-practice companion for children who stammer — and an honest account of the reasoning behind every part of it.

Ages ~5–10 Free, no account Works offline Nothing leaves the device

Before anything else: what this is not

It is not an assessment, it does not produce a severity score, and it is not a fluency-shaping programme. It cannot tell you whether a child is stuttering more or less this month.

It is the bit between sessions — the daily few minutes of practice that a clinician prescribes and a family then has to somehow make happen, sixty times before the next appointment. Everything here is built to make a child want to open it tomorrow, while practising something that is actually worth practising.

How it started, and why that matters

The design of this app is not a feature list someone brainstormed. It is a list of repairs.

It was built by a parent for their own six-year-old son, who stammers. The first version had all the obvious ingredients: breathing exercises, a coin economy, an avatar to unlock, a streak counter, a set of practice games. He loved it for about a fortnight.

Then he stopped opening it. He never said why — children rarely do. He just drifted off, and the app sat on the home screen going stale.

Rather than adding more features, the next step was to work out precisely what had gone wrong. Five things had, and each one is a trap that any practice app can fall into:

  • The rewards had nothing to do with speaking. He worked out he could earn the entire shop by tapping buttons in silence. The app was paying him for compliance, not for using his voice.
  • The streak was punitive. One missed day wiped out weeks of work. For a child, that converts a source of pride into a source of dread — and the safest way to stop losing a streak is to stop caring about it.
  • Nothing came back from the adults. He practised into a void. The parent dashboard existed but was fed by data that was never actually recorded.
  • The content ran out. Around sixty-five fixed items. He had seen all of them.
  • Progress was invisible. He had genuinely improved, and there was no way for him to hear or see it. The only visible number was coins, which he already knew were meaningless.

That diagnosis became the specification. Every feature below traces back to one of those five failures, or to a clinical target worth aiming at once a child is actually turning up.

The one question every feature is judged against: does this give the child a reason to open the app tomorrow, while practising something that helps? A feature that only satisfies the second half is a feature nobody uses.

Two decisions that shape everything else

If you only read two things on this page, read these. They explain most of what looks unusual about the app.

1. It listens to the voice, not the words

The obvious way to build a speech app is speech recognition. This app deliberately does not use it, and that decision should not be quietly reversed by anyone extending it.

Speech recognition scores word accuracy. It treats repetitions, prolongations and blocks as garbage input. Pointing that at a child who stammers builds exactly the wrong feedback loop — the app would systematically tell him he was failing at the precise moments he most needed encouragement.

Instead the app analyses the shape of the sound using the browser's audio analyser. Three things are measured, none of which care what word was said or whether it came out cleanly:

  • Is the voice on? Loudness against a threshold calibrated to this child in this room.
  • How long is the voice sustained? Continuous voicing, for the breath and stretching work.
  • How gently does a word begin? The time the sound takes to rise from 10% to 90% of its level — a proxy for hard versus easy vocal onset. A hard glottal attack rises in roughly 15 ms; an easy onset takes 110 ms or more. Crucially this measure is independent of loudness, so a shy child and a loud child are judged on the same thing.

A repetition, a prolongation or a block therefore costs the child nothing. The app has no concept of a mispronounced word, so it can never punish one.

2. Nothing can be earned in silence

Every scoring activity is gated on the microphone actually hearing the child. Coins arrive because a sound was made and held, not because a button was pressed. The animation of a word stretching apart is driven by the child's own sustained voicing — it will not move if he watches in silence.

This is the single most important repair, and it also protects the parent dashboard: a number that went up means the child spoke.

The corollary — the microphone is never a wall. If mic access is refused or unavailable, the app still opens, and the features that do not need a mic still work fully. A child should never be locked out of his own practice app by a permission dialog.

The features, and the reason for each

Grouped by the job they do rather than by where they appear in the menus.

Getting the app to hear this particular child

A fixed loudness threshold is the quiet failure that kills voice-driven apps. Set it too high and a softly spoken child is ignored, no matter how hard he tries. Set it too low and room noise earns his rewards for him.

🎤 Teach Me Your Voice

WhyThe app needs to know what this child sounds like, and how noisy his room is, before it can judge anything. Asking a parent to tune a sensitivity slider was never going to happen.
What it isFour short steps played as a game rather than presented as settings: a mouse voice, a normal voice, a lion roar, and one gently started word. The app measures the room's noise floor and his own quiet and loud levels, then sets its detection threshold between the room and his softest voice.
What it givesDetection that works for a quiet child. In testing this moved one child from a marginal margin to a comfortable one — the difference between an app that responds and an app that appears broken. It can be re-run at any time from the parent dashboard, which matters as a child grows or moves to a noisier room.

Games that each target one specific mechanism

Four games, four different targets. None of them scores fluency. Each gives immediate, visible feedback on a physical behaviour a child can consciously change — which is the only kind of target worth setting for a six-year-old.

🌱 Gentle Start

WhyEasy vocal onset — beginning a word softly instead of forcing it out from a closed larynx — is one of the few techniques a young child can genuinely feel themselves doing.
What it isA word appears; the child says it. The app measures how gradually his voice arrived and shows it back to him. Because a synthesised voice cannot demonstrate a soft onset, the app plays a purpose-built tone that swells gently, so he has something to imitate.
What it givesThe one number in the app that tracks a real speech target. The goal ratchets upward from his own recent measurements rather than sitting at a fixed ideal, so it is always just above where he actually is.

🌈 Stretchy Speech

WhyProlonged, continuous voicing is incompatible with a block. Practising the sensation of a sound that keeps going builds the alternative to pushing.
What it isHold a sound; the app tracks how long the voice stays continuously on, tolerating brief natural gaps rather than snapping off at the first flicker.
A personal best he can beat. Progress here is measured in tenths of a second and is entirely his own — nobody else's score is on the screen.

⏸️ Pause Power

WhyTime pressure is the enemy. A child who believes a gap in his speech is a failure will rush, and rushing makes stammering worse. So the app makes deliberate silence the thing that wins.
What it isSpeak, then stop. The app times the real silence between two real stretches of speech and rewards the length of the pause.
Reframing. A pause stops being something to be ashamed of and becomes a superpower with a high score attached.

🦕 My Buddy Nothing scored

WhyAll the work above is about speaking more easily. None of it addresses how a child feels about getting stuck — and avoidance and shame do more long-term damage than the disfluency itself. This is the acceptance and desensitisation side, which almost no children's app attempts.
What it isA named creature who stammers, is entirely fine about it, and needs the child's help. It gets stuck on a word and he chooses how to help. All the options are valid — there is no wrong answer and no score — and one of them is deliberate, playful bouncing on a sound ("b-b-bouncing"), which is voluntary stuttering introduced as a game.
The lines the buddy says are the actual payload. "I got a bit stuck… and I said it anyway." "Thanks for waiting for me — people who wait are the best." The second one quietly teaches a child what he is allowed to ask of a listener. Being the helper rather than the helped is the point: it is a rehearsal of a stance towards his own speech.

Tools for the moment itself

Practice is not much use if none of it is reachable when a word is actually stuck.

✨ Word Stretch

WhyA direct rehearsal of what to do with a word that will not come: breathe, slow down, stretch it out.
What it isThe letters of the word physically pull apart as the child sustains the sound. His voice is doing the animating — this was once a button that animated itself and paid out coins for being watched, which is exactly the bug the whole app was rebuilt to remove.
Cause and effect he can see. Longer voicing, wider letters. No interpretation needed.

🌬️ Calm Breath

WhyTension is upstream of everything else. A three-minute guided breath before practice changes the body a child is about to speak with, and gives him something to do when he feels the tightening start.
What it isAn animated breathing guide, roughly three minutes, no microphone required.
A portable skill. It is also the one activity that works when a child is too upset to want to talk at all.

🎵 Pacing Dots

WhySyllable-timed and rhythmic speech is long-established practice material. The catch is that it is trivially fakeable — a child can tap along in silence.
What it isA row of dots lights in rhythm; a beat only counts if he actually voiced it.
Rhythm and slowed rate, with no way to earn it by tapping.

His own words, in his own voice

Autonomy is one of the three things that make any activity intrinsically motivating — the others being competence and connection. An app that only ever hands a child someone else's word list is missing the cheapest motivation available.

✍️ My Words

WhyThe words that matter are the ones that trip him up: his own name, his teacher's name, the thing he has to say at the school gate. A generic list cannot know them, and a clinician's list is only updated fortnightly.
What it isHe adds his own difficult words, and records himself saying each one well. Those words then flow into the practice games, and his own recording becomes the model the app plays back — falling back to a synthesised voice only when he has not recorded one.
Two things at once. Practice on material that actually matters, and the experience of hearing his own voice held up as the good example. He is not copying an adult; he is copying himself on a good day.

🎲 Content that does not run out

WhyThe original app had a fixed set of items and he exhausted it. Nothing erodes a daily habit faster than a child recognising every screen.
What it isWords and prompts generated from word banks, seeded by the date: the same material all day so he can come back to a word he wants another go at, different material tomorrow. The clinical constraints live in the banks rather than being left to chance — the gentle-onset words all begin with vowels, and the words used for stretching are built on sounds that can physically be prolonged.
An app that stays new without an adult loading content into it.

Other people — where transfer actually happens

Fluency in a game and fluency in a conversation are different skills. There is normally a chasm between practising words alone and talking to a real human, and the app used to have nothing in it.

💛 Talk Together Turns only, never fluency

WhyConversation is where communicative pressure does the most damage, and it is also the only place practice becomes useful. It needs a rung on the ladder of its own.
What it isStructured turn-taking with a real person in the room — a parent, sibling, grandparent or clinician. The app supplies the prompts and keeps the turns moving.
A scaffolded conversation. It counts turns taken and nothing else. No score for how the words came out, deliberately: the aim is that speaking with someone stays entirely consequence-free. It also works completely with the microphone denied, because the microphone is not the point here.

🌍 Real Challenges

WhyAvoidance is the habit that hardens over a lifetime. Small, chosen, real acts of speaking are how it is loosened, and they belong outside the app.
What it isDaily suggestions of brave everyday tries — ordering his own drink, answering the phone, asking a shopkeeper a question — which he marks off himself.
Practice that leaves the screen. Credit is for the attempt, never for how it sounded.

Making progress visible to the child

He had improved and could not tell. A child who cannot perceive his own progress has no evidence that any of this is worth doing, and coins are not evidence — he knows they are made up.

🎧 Voice Journal — Then vs Now

WhyThe most convincing proof available is the sound of his own voice a month ago.
What it isRecordings are kept and grouped by prompt, so the same sentence from his earliest attempt and his latest sit side by side to be played back to back.
Audible progress — for him, and for the adult who needs to know whether any of this is working. This is also the part families most want to keep, which is why the app takes storage seriously (see below).

⚡ My Voice Powers

WhyRaw milliseconds mean nothing to a child. But levelling up a named power means a great deal.
What it isFour powers — Gentle Start, Long Sound, Pause Power, Steady Beat — each levelling up from his own real measurements, described in words he can act on rather than numbers.
Legible competence. The growth he sees here is genuinely his own performance, not a participation trophy. That distinction is what makes it feel worth something.

🎙️ Record Me!

WhyMany children who stammer have never simply listened to themselves in a setting where nothing is at stake.
What it isRecord a prompt, play it back, keep it. No analysis, no score.
Self-monitoring without judgement, and the raw material for Then vs Now.

Coming back tomorrow

The hardest problem in the whole app, and where the original version failed worst.

🛣️ Confidence Road

WhyThe original streak punished a missed day by destroying the record, which made the streak frightening. Illness, holidays and bad days are not lapses in commitment; a design that treats them as failures teaches a child that consistency is impossible.
What it isA cumulative road of every day ever practised — it only ever grows. Alongside it, a current streak that is protected by rest credits: practise consistently and the app banks days off, so a missed day spends a credit before it breaks anything.
A record that is safe to care about. A child can come back after a fortnight away and find his history intact, which is exactly the moment most apps lose someone for good.

😄 Emotion check-in and check-out

WhyHow a child feels about talking is the outcome that matters most and is the least measurable. Asking either side of practice makes it visible — and asking at all tells him his feelings are part of this.
What it isA five-face scale before practice and again afterwards.
A trend line the adults can see, and a moment of being asked. In testing, practice reliably ended with a better face than it started with; that pattern is worth showing a parent who is wondering whether the effort is worth it.

🪙 Coins, Rewards Shop and Avatar

WhyExtrinsic reward gets a child through the door in week one, and it is the weakest of the motivators here — offered too freely it actively erodes the interest it was meant to create. So it is present, capped, and never the main event.
What it isCoins for voiced practice, spent on unlockable avatars and customisation. Earnings taper per activity per day, so grinding one favourite game stops paying and breadth is rewarded. A first-practice-of-the-day bonus does the rest of the work.
A gentle pull towards turning up, without teaching him that speaking is something one does for payment. Several of the newest features pay nothing at all, on purpose.

For the adult in the room

A parent or clinician needs three things: is he actually using it, is anything moving, and what should I do differently.

👨‍👩‍👧 Parent Dashboard

WhyThe original dashboard was fed by data that was never recorded, so it showed a confident and completely empty picture. A dashboard that cannot be trusted is worse than none.
What it isActivities completed per day for the past week; the before-and-after emotion trend; a gentle-starts trend showing his median onset rise time when he began versus recently, against his current goal; recent sessions; a rotating coaching tip; and a plain statement of where his data is stored and whether it is protected.
An honest picture, including when it is flat. The gentle-starts panel says "holding steady for now — this one moves slowly, and that is normal" rather than inventing progress. Onset change is a slow measure and a dashboard that flattered it would be useless to a clinician.

💡 Coaching tips

WhyMost of the variance in a child's day sits with how the adults around him respond, and much of the standard advice runs against a parent's instincts — not finishing his sentences, not looking away, not asking him to slow down or take a breath.
What it isOne short, actionable prompt each visit, in the place a parent already looks.
Reinforcement of the listener's role, at the moment they are already thinking about their child's speech.

What is deliberately not measured

Some of the omissions are the design.

  • Word accuracy or intelligibility. No speech recognition anywhere, for the reason set out above.
  • Fluency, in any form. Nothing in the app counts disfluencies, and nothing scores how smoothly a word came out. Blocks and repetitions cost nothing.
  • Conversation quality. Talk Together counts turns and stops there.
  • Anything at all in My Buddy. The acceptance work is unscored on purpose — attaching a reward to it would turn a stance into a performance.
  • Comparison with other children. There is no leaderboard, no age norm, no percentile. Every measure is against his own past.

The tone rules follow from this. No red, no buzzers, no failure sounds anywhere in the app. Getting stuck is drawn as ordinary and survivable rather than as an error state. Praise is for effort and for turning up — "I noticed you kept going" — and never for fluency.

Where the data lives

Short version: on the device, and nowhere else.

  • No account, no sign-up, no server. There is nothing to log in to.
  • Recordings never leave the device. A child's voice is about as sensitive as data gets. The recordings are stored in the browser's own database on that one device and are never uploaded, transmitted or analysed elsewhere.
  • No analytics, no tracking, no third-party scripts.
  • Works fully offline once loaded, which is also why none of this needs a connection.

The honest caveat. Because everything is local, everything is also only on that one device. Browsers can clear this kind of storage after a stretch without opening the app — taking the streak and the recordings with it. The app asks the browser for permanent storage at startup, and adding it to the home screen makes eviction far less likely; the parent dashboard reports which state you are actually in. But there is currently no sync and no cloud backup, so a lost or wiped device means lost recordings. Worth knowing before a family invests months of Then-vs-Now in it.

For speech and language therapists

If you are considering pointing a family at this, here is what to look at and what to be wary of.

A ten-minute tour

  1. Open the app and run Teach Me Your Voice first. Nothing else behaves properly until it has calibrated, and it is the piece most likely to need re-running in a noisy clinic room.
  2. Play Gentle Start three or four times, once with a hard attack and once with a deliberately eased onset, and watch what it reports. This is the app's only real speech measure and you should satisfy yourself it responds sensibly.
  3. Open My Buddy. It is the feature that will tell you fastest whether the app's attitude to stammering matches yours.
  4. Open Talk Together and take a couple of turns with the child or parent. This is the part designed to be used with someone, and the part most likely to be skipped if nobody points it out.
  5. Finish in the Parent Dashboard so you know what the family will see, and can tell them which parts to ignore.

How to read the one clinical number

The gentle-starts panel reports median onset rise time in milliseconds — how long the voice takes to climb from 10% to 90% of its level at the start of a word.

What the goal tracks

It is computed from a rolling window of the child's own samples, and the goal is set just above his recent median rather than at a fixed ideal. Treat it as a practice-behaviour measure, not an outcome measure: it tells you whether he is rehearsing easy onset, not whether his stammering has changed.

Known limits, stated plainly

  • A single amplitude threshold cannot separate a child's voice from a sibling shouting nearby. In a busy room, credit can be earned by the room.
  • Onset rise time is a proxy. It is a good one, it is loudness-invariant, and it is not a laryngoscope.
  • The content banks are English and assume a British-English register in the child-facing copy.
  • The reading load is pitched at roughly ages 5–10 and the visual register is young; an older child may find it babyish.
  • No sync, no multi-device, no clinician view, no data export yet. What you see on the child's device is all there is.
  • It has been shaped around one child in depth rather than trialled across a caseload. That is a real limitation and the main reason clinician feedback is worth more than another feature.

What would help most. Whether the targets are the right ones, whether anything here would work against what you do in session, and whether the buddy's framing of getting stuck sits right with you. Those are the judgements this app cannot make for itself.
